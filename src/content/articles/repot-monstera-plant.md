Knowing **how to repot a Monstera plant** helps you refresh old potting mix, give crowded roots more room and keep a large climbing plant stable. Repotting is not something you need to do every year automatically. Wait until the roots have filled the container, the mix has broken down, or the plant is becoming difficult to water or keep upright.

You can sometimes split a multi-stem Monstera while repotting, but only when the plant has separate rooted sections that can survive on their own. Do not pull a single vine into pieces or divide it just because it has several leaves. Each division needs enough roots and a viable stem node or growing point.

This guide focuses on *Monstera deliciosa*. The same basic principles apply to many climbing houseplants, but root structure and growth habits vary. For everyday watering and light, see our [Monstera plant care guide](/articles/monstera-plant-care/), or start with the full [Monstera plant guide](/articles/monstera-plant-guide/).

## When does a Monstera need repotting?

Look for a combination of signs rather than relying on a calendar. A Monstera may need a new pot when:

- Roots are growing from the drainage holes or circling densely around the root ball.
- The pot dries much faster than it used to, even when light and temperature have not changed.
- The plant has become top-heavy and tips easily because the root ball fills the container.
- Water runs straight through the pot because the mix has broken down, shrunk or become difficult to re-wet.
- The potting mix stays wet for an unusually long time because it is compacted or poorly structured.
- Growth has slowed and the plant is clearly root-bound, although slow growth alone is not proof that a larger pot is needed.

A Monstera can remain in the same pot for a while if the roots are healthy and the mix still drains well. Repotting too often can disturb roots unnecessarily. The University of Minnesota Extension's [Monstera propagation and repotting guidance](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/propagating-monstera-deliciosa) also lists crowded roots, rapid drying and an unstable plant among reasons to move to a larger pot.

## Choose the right pot and potting mix

Move up by one pot size rather than choosing a much larger container. As a practical guide, the new pot is often about 2–5 cm wider than the current root ball. The roots should have some fresh space around them, but they should not sit inside a large volume of unused, wet mix.

Drainage holes are essential. If you like a decorative cachepot without holes, keep the Monstera in a separate nursery pot with drainage and remove it for watering. Let it drain before returning it to the outer pot. Do not leave water pooled at the bottom.

Use a loose, airy potting mix. A blend of quality indoor potting mix, orchid bark and perlite or pumice can provide both moisture retention and air spaces. The exact proportions can be adjusted to your room and watering habits. If your home is cool or the plant tends to stay wet, use a more open mix. If the room is hot and the pot dries quickly, a slightly more moisture-retentive blend may be appropriate.

Avoid heavy garden soil in a container. It can compact and hold too much water around indoor roots. Do not add a layer of gravel to the bottom as a substitute for drainage holes; use a pot that drains properly throughout its base.

## What to prepare before you start

Gather the new pot, fresh mix, clean scissors or pruning shears, gloves and a clean surface. If the plant is tall, ask someone to support the stems while you work. A mature *M. deliciosa* can be awkward to handle because its leaves and stems are heavy and its aerial roots may be attached to a pole.

Watering the plant a day before repotting can make the root ball easier to remove if the mix is very dry, but do not start with a saturated pot. If the soil is already wet, wait until it has drained and is easier to handle. Remove any ties that would pull against the stems, but keep the support nearby if the plant will need it again.

Wear gloves if you have sensitive skin. Monstera tissue contains calcium oxalate crystals and should not be chewed by people or pets. Keep cuttings and loose plant material away from animals; see our [Monstera pet safety guide](/articles/monstera-toxic-to-cats-and-dogs/).

## Step-by-step: how to repot a Monstera plant

### 1. Remove the plant carefully

Lay the pot on its side or support the plant while easing the root ball out. Squeeze a flexible nursery pot gently to loosen the sides. Do not pull the plant out by its leaf stalks. If roots have grown through the drainage holes, free them carefully or cut the old pot if it cannot be removed without tearing the root system.

### 2. Inspect the roots

Healthy roots are usually firm. Their colour can vary with the potting mix and age, so do not judge by colour alone. Soft, mushy roots that break apart easily or smell unpleasant may be rotting. Remove only roots that are clearly dead or damaged, using clean scissors. Avoid cutting healthy roots simply to make the plant fit a pot that is too small.

If most roots are healthy, keep the root ball largely intact. Loosen only the outer layer if it is tightly circling. If the old mix is compacted, gently tease some away without stripping every root bare. The aim is to refresh the root environment while avoiding unnecessary disturbance.

### 3. Add fresh mix to the new pot

Place a small amount of mix in the bottom so the plant will sit at the same height as before. Set the root ball in the centre and check the stem base. The stem should not be buried deeper than it was in the old pot. Add mix around the sides, working it into gaps with your fingers. Do not pack it down hard; the mix needs air spaces.

### 4. Settle the plant and water

Once the pot is filled, water slowly until excess drains from the holes. This settles the mix around the roots. Top up any large gaps if the level falls, but avoid burying the stem base. Empty the saucer or cachepot after the water has drained.

### 5. Return the plant to a stable position

Place the Monstera in bright, indirect light and avoid a sudden change in temperature. Keep the support in place or reinstall it so the stem does not wobble. Do not feed immediately after repotting; fresh mix may already contain nutrients, and disturbed roots need time to settle. Resume normal feeding during active growth according to the fertiliser label.

## How to split a Monstera plant

People often use “split” to mean dividing one large pot into two or more plants. This is possible when the pot contains multiple rooted stems or natural divisions. It is not the same as cutting a single vine into sections. A stem cutting can be propagated, but a division is a rooted part of the existing plant.

Before dividing, check the base of the plant and identify where separate stems emerge. Gently remove the root ball and look for natural gaps between root systems. If two or more stems have their own roots and nodes, you may be able to separate them by hand. Use clean shears to cut a small number of connecting roots or stems only when necessary. Do not force apart roots that are tightly intertwined.

Each division should have:

- A healthy stem with at least one viable node and growing point.
- Enough healthy roots to support the leaves it carries.
- At least one healthy leaf, where possible, to help the new section photosynthesise.
- A suitable pot and fresh, airy potting mix.

If one section has no roots, treat it as a cutting and follow [how to propagate a Monstera plant](/articles/propagate-monstera-plant/) instead of potting it as an established division. If there is only one rooted stem, do not try to split it into multiple plants simply to increase the number of pots.

## Aftercare: what to expect after repotting

Some temporary drooping can happen after root disturbance. Keep the plant in stable bright, indirect light and check moisture before watering again. The fresh mix should be moist after the initial watering, then allowed to dry at the top before the next watering. Do not keep it wet in the hope that roots will recover faster.

Avoid heavy pruning at the same time unless you need to remove damaged tissue. Repotting and severe pruning together can put extra stress on the plant. If several leaves begin turning yellow, check the moisture and roots before adding fertiliser. Our [yellow Monstera leaves guide](/articles/monstera-yellow-leaves/) explains how to investigate.

## Frequently asked questions about repotting and splitting Monstera

### What size pot should I use for a Monstera?

Choose a pot about 2–5 cm wider than the current root ball, with drainage holes. Moving up one size gives roots room without surrounding them with too much wet mix.

### How often should I repot a Monstera plant?

Repot when roots fill the container, the mix has broken down or the plant becomes unstable. Many plants need attention every year or two, but growth conditions and pot size vary, so use the plant's condition rather than a fixed schedule.

### Should I water a Monstera before repotting?

A slightly moist root ball is easier to handle than a bone-dry one, but the pot should not be saturated. If the mix is already wet, allow it to drain before repotting.

### Can I split one Monstera into two plants?

Yes, if the pot contains separate rooted stems or natural divisions. Each section needs healthy roots and a viable stem node or growing point. If you only have a stem section without roots, propagate it as a cutting.

### Can I divide a Monstera without damaging it?

Some root disturbance is unavoidable, but you can reduce stress by separating natural divisions gently, keeping healthy roots and using clean tools. Do not pull apart tightly connected sections by force.

### Should I remove old soil from Monstera roots?

Remove loose, compacted or damaged mix as needed, but you do not need to bare-root a healthy plant. Keep as much healthy root structure intact as practical.

### Why is my Monstera drooping after repotting?

The roots may be adjusting after disturbance, or the mix may be too wet or too dry. Check moisture, drainage and stem stability. Avoid adding fertiliser or watering repeatedly without checking the soil.

### Can I repot a Monstera in winter?

You can repot if there is a genuine problem, such as root rot or a broken pot. For routine repotting, spring or early active growth is usually easier because the plant can resume growth more readily.

### Can I use ordinary potting soil for Monstera?

A quality indoor potting mix can be part of the blend, but dense soil alone may retain too much water. Add an airy component such as orchid bark and perlite or pumice, and use a pot with drainage.
