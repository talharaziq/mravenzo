To understand **how to propagate a Monstera plant**, start with a stem cutting that includes a node and a viable growth point. This is the most reliable method for producing a new plant from a healthy Monstera. A leaf by itself is not enough. The node is the part of the stem where new roots and shoots can develop, so a cutting without one may stay green for a while but will not grow into a complete new plant.

You can root a Monstera cutting in water, directly in a suitable potting mix, or by air layering while the stem is still attached to the parent plant. Each method can work. Water lets you see root development, soil avoids transferring water roots into a new medium, and air layering can be useful when you want a large cutting with established roots before separating it.

This guide focuses on *Monstera deliciosa*. For plant care after propagation, see our [Monstera plant care guide](/articles/monstera-plant-care/) and the broader [Monstera plant guide](/articles/monstera-plant-guide/). A mature cutting may need [climbing support](/articles/support-monstera-plant/) as it establishes, and the [repotting guide](/articles/repot-monstera-plant/) explains when to move it into a larger container.

## What you need before taking a cutting

Prepare clean, sharp pruning shears or a knife, a clean glass or small pot, and either water or a lightly moist propagation mix. If you use water, choose a clear container so you can check the roots. If you use soil, choose a small pot with drainage holes and a loose medium that holds some moisture but does not remain saturated.

Wear gloves if you have sensitive skin and keep cut plant tissue away from children and pets. *Monstera deliciosa* contains insoluble calcium oxalate crystals and is listed as toxic to cats and dogs by the ASPCA. Dispose of unused sections securely.

## Find a Monstera node before cutting

Look along the stem for a node. It is usually visible as a joint or slightly raised point where a leaf stalk attaches. You may see a small aerial root emerging from the same area. The space between two nodes is called an internode.

A viable cutting needs at least one node with an axillary bud or growth point. A leaf blade and its petiole without a node will not develop into a new vine. The University of Minnesota Extension states that Monstera leaf and petiole sections without a node and axillary bud will not produce new growth. Its [propagation guide](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/propagating-monstera-deliciosa) includes a visual explanation of where to cut.

For a first attempt, choose a healthy section with one node, one leaf and, if available, a short aerial root. A cutting with more than one node may offer more than one potential growth point, but it is also larger and may need more space in a propagation container.

## Step-by-step: take a stem cutting

1. Choose a healthy section of stem and identify the node you want to keep on the cutting.
2. Check that the cutting includes a viable bud or growing point. Do not choose a leaf-only section.
3. Use clean, sharp shears to cut the stem below the node, leaving the node on the cutting.
4. Remove any leaf that would sit below the waterline or inside the potting mix. Keep the node accessible.
5. Let the cut surface settle briefly while you prepare the rooting container. Do not leave the cutting to dry out for a long time.
6. Place the cutting in water or propagation mix using one of the methods below.

If the parent plant becomes too long after taking the cutting, you can make a clean cut near a suitable node and train the remaining stem onto a support. Read [how to prune a Monstera plant](/articles/prune-monstera-plant/) before making several cuts.

## Method 1: propagate a Monstera in water

Water propagation is popular because you can see when roots form. Fill a clean glass with room-temperature water and place the node in the water. Keep the leaf and most of the petiole above the waterline. If the cutting has an aerial root, it can sit in the water too, but make sure the leaf does not become submerged.

Set the glass in bright, indirect light in a warm room. Avoid direct sun on the glass, which can heat the water and encourage algae. Check the water level regularly and refresh it when it becomes cloudy or develops an unpleasant smell. Rinse the glass if residue builds up.

Roots may appear over several weeks, but the timing depends on warmth, light and the condition of the cutting. Do not keep lifting the cutting out to inspect it. Once several healthy roots have formed and are growing, you can transfer it to a small pot with a free-draining mix. Rather than using a fixed root-length rule, look for a small network of roots that can support the cutting in the pot.

After potting, keep the mix lightly and evenly moist while the cutting adjusts, but never waterlog it. Water roots and soil roots develop under different conditions, so some older roots may change after transfer. A little adjustment is normal; a persistently wet pot is not.

## Method 2: propagate directly in potting mix

Direct soil propagation avoids a water-to-soil transfer. Fill a small pot with a loose, lightly moistened propagation mix. Make a hole for the stem so the node can sit against or just below the surface, depending on the shape of the cutting. Keep the leaf above the mix and make sure the node is in contact with the medium.

Firm the mix gently around the stem so the cutting does not wobble. Place the pot in bright, indirect light and keep it warm. The medium should remain lightly moist, not wet. If it dries completely, root development may stall; if it stays saturated, the node can rot.

You can use a clear propagation box or loose humidity cover if the air is very dry, but provide some ventilation. Condensation should not remain heavy all day, and leaves should not be pressed against a wet cover. Check the cutting periodically for firm tissue and new growth rather than pulling it out repeatedly.

A new leaf is a useful sign that the cutting is growing, but do not assume that the first small root automatically means it is ready for normal houseplant care. Allow the roots to develop enough to hold the plant in the mix.

## Method 3: air-layer a Monstera before cutting

Air layering can reduce the risk of a cutting failing because the stem develops roots while it is still attached to the parent plant. It is useful for a long vine or a valuable cutting that you do not want to separate before roots form.

Choose a healthy node with an aerial root or a point where roots can develop. Surround the node with lightly moist sphagnum moss, then wrap the moss loosely in clear plastic or an air-layering sleeve. Secure the ends so the moss stays in place, but do not crush the stem. Keep the moss lightly moist and check it periodically.

When a visible network of roots has formed around the node, cut the stem below the rooted section with clean shears. Pot the new plant in an airy mix and support it if needed. Do not leave the moss waterlogged; saturated moss can encourage rot. If the stem becomes soft or discoloured, remove the wrap and inspect it.

## When is a Monstera cutting ready for potting?

For a water cutting, wait until it has several healthy roots rather than transferring it as soon as the first root appears. For air layering, wait for a visible root network. For soil propagation, keep the cutting in place until new growth and resistance to a very gentle tug suggest that roots have formed. Avoid pulling hard because new roots are fragile.

Choose a pot only a little larger than the rooted section. A large pot holds too much unused moisture around a small root system. Use a mix that drains freely and a container with holes. Keep the new plant in bright, indirect light and avoid fertilising until it has settled and is actively growing.

## Common Monstera propagation mistakes

**Taking a leaf without a node:** This is the most common error. A leaf can remain green for a time, but it will not produce a new vine without a growth point.

**Submerging the leaf:** Keep the node in water, not the entire leaf. Submerged leaves can decay and make the water foul.

**Using a pot that is too large:** A small cutting in a big pot can remain wet for too long. Choose a container that matches the developing root system.

**Keeping the medium soaked:** Propagation needs moisture and oxygen. Wet, airless medium can cause the node and roots to rot.

**Changing the cutting's environment repeatedly:** Keep it warm and in bright indirect light. Avoid moving it between hot sun, cold windows and different propagation methods.

**Expecting instant growth:** Rooting takes time, and a cutting may pause while it develops roots. Be patient and inspect it gently.

## Frequently asked questions about Monstera propagation

### Can you propagate a Monstera from one leaf?

A leaf and petiole without a stem node will not grow into a complete new plant. Choose a stem cutting with at least one node and a viable growth point.

### How long does Monstera propagation take?

It can take several weeks or longer for roots to develop. Warmth, light, cutting health and the propagation method all affect timing. Look for healthy root growth rather than relying on a guaranteed number of days.

### Is it better to propagate Monstera in water or soil?

Both methods can work. Water lets you see root development, while soil avoids transferring water-grown roots into a different medium. Choose the method you can keep consistently warm, bright and appropriately moist.

### Where do I cut a Monstera for propagation?

Identify a stem node with a growth point and cut below it so the node stays on the cutting. Do not cut off only a leaf and petiole.

### Can I propagate a Monstera with an aerial root?

Yes. A cutting with a node and aerial root is useful, but the aerial root is not a substitute for the node. Keep the node in contact with water or propagation mix.

### Should I change Monstera propagation water?

Refresh it when it becomes cloudy or dirty and top it up as needed. Use a clean container and keep the leaf above the waterline.

### Why is my Monstera cutting rotting?

Possible causes include a submerged leaf, stagnant water, a saturated propagation mix, cold conditions or a damaged node. Remove decaying tissue if a healthy node remains, use clean equipment and restart in a clean container or fresh medium.

### Can I propagate a Monstera in winter?

It is possible, but rooting may be slower when light and temperatures are low. A warm, bright indoor position can help. Avoid taking large cuttings from a plant that is already stressed.

### When should I move a rooted cutting into a larger pot?

Move it when the roots have established and the current pot is becoming crowded or dries very quickly. Increase the pot size gradually rather than placing a small cutting in a large container.
