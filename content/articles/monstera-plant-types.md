There are several **Monstera plant types**, and they do not all look like the large Swiss cheese plant seen in home décor photographs. Some have broad leaves with deep splits, some have smaller leaves full of holes, and others have narrow, unfenestrated leaves with white or cream markings. A few plants sold as “Monstera” are actually different genera.

The easiest way to identify a plant is to start with its botanical name, then compare leaf shape, growth habit and variegation. Common names can overlap between nurseries and countries. This guide covers the plants most likely to appear in a houseplant collection, explains the difference between Thai Constellation and Albo, and clears up the Mini Monstera and Cobra names.

If you are choosing a plant for your home, our [Monstera plant guide](/articles/monstera-plant-guide/) covers general growing conditions. For ongoing care, see [how to care for a Monstera plant](/articles/monstera-plant-care/). For climbing varieties, read [how to support a Monstera plant](/articles/support-monstera-plant/); if you plan to make new plants, see [how to propagate a Monstera](/articles/propagate-monstera-plant/).

## 1. Monstera deliciosa: the classic Swiss cheese plant

*Monstera deliciosa* is the familiar large-leaved species. Young plants have smooth, heart-shaped leaves. As the plant matures, new leaves can develop deep cuts along the edges and oval holes inside the leaf blade. The stem is thick, and the plant climbs by producing aerial roots.

A mature *M. deliciosa* can become very large in a suitable greenhouse or tropical garden. Indoors, its size depends on the light, pot, support and length of time it has been growing. It is a better choice for someone with room for a climbing plant than for a narrow shelf.

A young *M. deliciosa* may not have holes yet. Do not identify it as a different species based only on an unfenestrated juvenile leaf. North Carolina State Extension describes the species as a climbing evergreen native from Mexico to Panama and notes its large mature leaves. See the [extension plant profile](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/monstera-deliciosa/).

## 2. Monstera deliciosa ‘Thai Constellation’

Thai Constellation is a variegated form of *M. deliciosa*. Its dark green leaves are marked with many cream or pale-yellow speckles and patches, often creating a marbled or starry appearance. The name refers to the pattern, not to a separate Monstera species.

The pale sections contain less chlorophyll than green tissue, so a heavily variegated plant may grow more slowly than a fully green *M. deliciosa*. Give it bright, indirect light to support growth, but do not place it in harsh direct sun. Avoid keeping the soil constantly wet in an attempt to help it grow faster.

When comparing plants, look at the balance of green and pale tissue, the condition of the roots and the presence of new growth. A very pale leaf can be attractive, but a plant still needs enough healthy green tissue to sustain itself. For care, follow the same basic approach as other *M. deliciosa* plants, with particular attention to good light and conservative watering.

## 3. Monstera deliciosa Albo (white-variegated Monstera)

“Monstera Albo” is a common trade name for white-variegated *M. deliciosa*. Its leaves often show larger white sectors or bold white streaks rather than the fine speckling associated with Thai Constellation. The pattern is not identical on every leaf. One leaf may be mostly green with a white stripe, while another may have a large white section.

The term “white Monstera” is therefore a broad shopping phrase rather than the botanical name of a separate species. Ask for the full plant name and, where possible, a photograph of the actual plant rather than relying on a generic listing photograph.

White tissue does not contribute as much to photosynthesis as green tissue. A cutting that produces almost completely white leaves may struggle to support new growth. Bright indirect light is useful, but direct sun can damage the foliage. Keep the plant in an airy mix and do not overwater it. The University of Minnesota Extension notes that variegated forms of *M. deliciosa* include Thai Constellation and Albo-type plants in its [Monstera propagation resource](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/propagating-monstera-deliciosa).

## 4. Monstera adansonii: the Swiss cheese vine

*Monstera adansonii* has smaller, thinner leaves with distinctive holes within the leaf blade. The leaf edges are usually less deeply split than those of mature *M. deliciosa*. Its flexible stems can trail from a hanging pot or climb a narrow support.

This type is often easier to fit into a small room because it can be trained or trimmed as a vine. It still needs bright, indirect light and a potting mix that drains well. If the vine becomes long and sparse, check the light before cutting it back. Propagation from a healthy node is usually a practical way to make a fuller pot.

Do not assume every hole-leaved Monstera is *M. adansonii*. The rare *M. obliqua* is frequently confused with it in online listings. Unless a plant has reliable identification, treat dramatic product descriptions with caution.

## 5. Monstera standleyana: the plant sometimes called Cobra

*Monstera standleyana* is a climbing species with narrow, oval or lance-shaped leaves. Some forms have creamy white or yellow variegation. It is sometimes sold under the common name “Philodendron Cobra”, even though it is botanically a Monstera. Kew's Plants of the World Online lists *Monstera standleyana* as an accepted species; see [Kew's taxonomic entry](https://powo.science.kew.org/taxon/urn:lsid:ipni.org:names:163961-2).

A useful identification point is that the leaves do not develop the familiar large splits and holes of mature *M. deliciosa*. The plant is still a climber, so a small trellis or pole can help guide its stems. If a seller uses “Cobra” without a botanical name, ask for the scientific name before buying.

## 6. Mini Monstera: a lookalike from another genus

The plant sold as Mini Monstera is generally *Rhaphidophora tetrasperma*. It has smaller split leaves and a climbing habit, which is why it resembles a young *M. deliciosa*. However, it is not part of the Monstera genus.

This distinction matters because common names are not precise. If you are researching toxicity, propagation or plant identification, use the botanical name on the label. North Carolina State Extension provides a separate [profile for *Rhaphidophora tetrasperma*](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/rhaphidophora-tetrasperma/), which helps distinguish it from true Monsteras. It also notes that this lookalike can cause irritation if eaten, so keep it away from pets and children. Our [pet safety guide](/articles/monstera-toxic-to-cats-and-dogs/) covers *M. deliciosa* specifically; always check the exact plant species rather than assuming advice applies to every lookalike.

## 7. Monstera siltepecana and other collector species

Some collectors grow less common species such as *M. siltepecana*, *M. dubia* and *M. obliqua*. Their leaves and growth patterns differ from the large *M. deliciosa*. For example, *M. dubia* is known for juvenile leaves that lie close against a climbing surface, while *M. obliqua* is rare and often mislabelled. These plants are not necessary for a beginner's collection, and a seller's name alone is not enough to confirm an identity.

If you are considering a collector species, check the botanical name, ask about the source of the plant, and compare its features with a botanical or specialist reference. Do not pay a premium just because a listing uses the word “rare”.

## How to choose the right Monstera for your space

**For a large floor plant:** Choose *M. deliciosa* if you have room for a climbing stem and a support. Allow space for the leaves to spread; they can extend beyond the pot footprint.

**For variegated foliage:** Thai Constellation and Albo are forms of *M. deliciosa*. Choose based on the pattern you prefer and the amount of light you can provide. Both still need green tissue and healthy roots.

**For shelves or hanging planters:** *M. adansonii* is a flexible vine that can trail or climb. Keep the stems trimmed if they become too long for the space.

**For a compact split-leaf look:** *Rhaphidophora tetrasperma* is often sold as Mini Monstera, but remember that it is a different genus.

**For narrow variegated leaves:** Look at correctly labelled *M. standleyana*. Confirm the botanical name if the listing says Cobra.

Whatever type you select, check the potting mix, drainage, leaf undersides and roots before bringing it home. A healthy, correctly identified green plant is usually a better purchase than an expensive specimen with uncertain labels or damaged roots.

## Frequently asked questions about Monstera plant types

### What is the most common type of Monstera?

*Monstera deliciosa* is the best-known and most widely recognised houseplant Monstera. It develops large leaves with splits and internal holes as it matures.

### Is Thai Constellation the same as Monstera Albo?

No. Both are variegated forms of *Monstera deliciosa*, but they usually have different patterns. Thai Constellation tends to show creamy speckles and marbling, while Albo commonly has larger white sectors or streaks.

### What is a white Monstera plant called?

“White Monstera” is a common description for white-variegated *M. deliciosa*, often sold as Monstera Albo. Check the botanical name because the phrase is not a precise scientific name.

### Is Mini Monstera a real Monstera?

No. Mini Monstera usually refers to *Rhaphidophora tetrasperma*. It is a related climbing aroid, but it belongs to a different genus.

### What is a Monstera Cobra plant?

The name Cobra is commonly used for variegated *Monstera standleyana*, also sold as Philodendron Cobra. Confirm the scientific name because common plant names can be inconsistent.

### Which Monstera is best for beginners?

A green *M. deliciosa* or *M. adansonii* is a practical starting point if you can provide bright indirect light, drainage and suitable watering. A fully green plant is often more forgiving than a highly variegated collector plant.

### Why does my Monstera deliciosa have no holes?

It may be juvenile, or it may be growing in light that is too low. Mature leaves usually develop more fenestrations under suitable conditions. Give the plant time and bright indirect light.

### Are variegated Monsteras harder to care for?

They need the same basic care as green *M. deliciosa*, but they often grow more slowly and need a brighter position. Their pale leaf sections are more vulnerable to scorch, so avoid strong direct sun.

### Is Monstera adansonii the same as Monstera obliqua?

No. They are separate species. *M. obliqua* is rare and often misidentified in commerce, so do not rely on a listing title or unusually large leaf holes alone.
